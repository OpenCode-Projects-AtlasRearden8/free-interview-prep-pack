# How to use the interview pack, sheet by sheet

Plain English, no jargon you didn't already have. Each section covers one
sheet: what it's for, when to use it, and the one habit that makes it work.

The rule for all of them: **rough handwriting beats perfect silence.** These
pages are drafts for a real conversation, not homework that gets graded.

## Star story worksheet

**When:** a quiet evening, several days before the interview.

Interviewers lean on "tell me about a time when..." questions. Instead of
writing speeches, you'll collect three true stories and shape them with
STAR: **Situation** (what was happening, two sentences max), **Task** (what
*you* were there to do), **Action** (what you actually did, step by step,
said with "I" and not "we"), and **Result** (what happened next; if there's
a number, the number gets the spotlight).

The trick that makes this easy: don't start from the question. Start from
your life. Once a story is shaped, it answers half a dozen different
questions — that's what the "this story works great for questions like…"
box is for.

## Practice question cards

**When:** the evenings in between. Twenty minutes, two cards, done.

Print, cut along the dashed lines, and practice **out loud** — thinking an
answer and saying it are different muscles, and the interview only hires the
second one. Then score yourself with the small scale at the bottom of each
card:

1. it came out rough (fine — that's how practice starts)
2. got there eventually
3. solid
4. ready to say in the room

A "1" isn't a grade; it's tomorrow's homework. Write **one** thing to tweak
next time, not three — one improvement per night is plenty, and it sticks.

## Company research sheet

**When:** midweek, twenty unhurried minutes. Stop while it's still
interesting.

Start with their own website and the full job posting, then recent news, and
only then public profiles. The most useful box is "the problem this role
exists to solve" — job postings are wish lists whispering a pain point. If
nobody needed help with something, the job wouldn't be open.

You do **not** have to solve the company. Fill what you can, and when you
get stuck, that's not a failure: turn the unknown into an interview question
on the questions-to-ask sheet instead.

## Questions to ask

**When:** pick three or four the night before; bring a spare.

Asking good questions is the most impressive thing available to you, because
it's proof you're taking this seriously in both directions. The sheet groups
them by topic — the work, the team, what success looks like, and the
practical stuff that's fine to ask directly. Skip anything the posting
already answered. And the "my own" lines are for the questions that appear
*mid-interview*; landing them is how interviews turn into conversations.

## Outfit and bag checklist

**When:** the night before, once.

Dress one notch above the job's normal day, then stop there. Comfortable
beats impressive — you need your attention for words, not for shoes. The
bag column handles the small gremlins: extra résumé copies, two pens, phone
silenced *and pocketed*, water, ID, directions. Check it, close the bag, and
then sleep the sleep of someone whose bag is already done.

## Day-of timeline

**When:** the night before (fill in your times), then resident by the door.

Pencil in what "leaving 15 minutes early," "arriving nearby to breathe," and
"walking in 10–15 minutes early" mean in your actual clock times. When each
step has a home, nothing lives in your head rent-free. If timing collapses
anyway, the fix is one calm phone call — the sheet includes the script.

## Post-interview reflection log

**When:** that evening, once, with a drink you like.

Circle one gut-check option honestly, then fill the few black lines: what
went better than expected, what was harder in person, the questions that
surprised you, and the words *they* used that mattered (their vocabulary
tells you what they care about — steal it politely for the thank-you and any
next round). One thing to prep differently next time. Then close the folder.
Grading one interview all night is how a decent day starts feeling like a
failure; the log exists so you don't have to keep re-running it in your head.

## Thank-you note (email)

**When:** within 24 hours, while the real moments are fresh.

Four sentences; one *specific* thing you enjoyed about talking with them;
done. Personal small-and-true beats grand-but-generic every send. Every
interviewer who spent time with you gets their own note — same structure,
different detail. And read it out loud once before sending; if it sounds
like a robot borrowing your name, un-formal it.

## Thank-you note (paper)

**When:** written the same or next day, mailed within two business days.

Nobody mails them anymore, which is exactly why the most memorable note on
the desk will be yours. Keep it short: thanks, one line they could only have
given you, one line that you're still interested, sign warmly. Legible beats
fancy, on the address too — print if your cursive got weather-polished.

## The whole pack, in one sentence

Show up prepared *at* yourself, not some braver fictional version of you —
the sheets exist so the nerves have less to do.
